Resonance doesn't pick sides

To connect distant planets to Earth's thermometers, L&S11 lean on resonance: the climate rings like a struck glass in sympathy with tiny astronomical nudges — while supposedly responding only sluggishly to the far larger push of CO₂. But resonance is a property of the system, not of the pusher it prefers. Here is a resonant system. Push it yourself.

The exercise: below is a simulated oscillator with a natural period of 60 years and a gentle ring — a stand-in for the claim's “resonant climate,” the same demonstration Benestad et al. ran in their supporting material. What this exercise shows. The metronome works — a small push at the right tempo builds a large swing. But so does everything else. Random, rhythm-free pushes excite the same 60-year ringing (which bears on Exercise 3: a 60-year oscillation in a record needs no 60-year cause). And the greenhouse ramp passes straight through: the system rides the rising push while it oscillates — trends and oscillations coexist, so finding an oscillation subtracts nothing from CO₂. A wine glass rings at its pitch for any sound at that pitch, whatever the source. A climate sensitive enough to amplify small planetary pushes cannot also be insensitive to the much larger push from CO₂ — yet that double standard is what L&S11's accounting requires. And even this concedes too much: the planetary push itself has no accepted physical mechanism behind it — the links in their chain (irradiance reconstructions, cosmic-ray cloud seeding) are themselves faulted elsewhere in the same replication study.

You have now run all five objections yourself: waves rebuild anything (Exercise 1); a 100-year window cannot certify a 60-year rhythm (Exercise 2); the fit “discovers” cycles in random data (Exercise 3); a close fit to the past does not imply predictive skill (Exercise 4); and resonance does not distinguish among causes (Exercise 5).
