The method finds cycles in pure randomness

This is the rebuttal's centerpiece, and you are about to run it with your own hands. What “true 60-yr content” means. The same fit, run on the whole 10,000-year series at once. The series is built from ~400 waves whose periods, strengths, and timings were all drawn at random — none was placed at 60 years on purpose. Across 167 repeats of a 60-year clock, only a wave that genuinely keeps 60-year time from end to end survives the averaging; the rest drift out of step and cancel. What survives is whatever accidental content sits within roughly ±0.4 years of 60 — close enough that even 10,000 years cannot tell it apart from 60 — and with this recipe that is typically zero or one modest wave out of the 400. That is why the dark line is tiny but not exactly zero. A 160-year slice holds fewer than three repeats, so nothing cancels — and since a fitted “cycle strength” can never be negative, every chance alignment counts upward. That is why the slices don't just scatter around the dark line; they sit above it.
What this exercise shows. Every slice “contains” a 60-year cycle — the fit has never once come back empty, because it can't: give it a wave to fit and it will fit a wave. The reported strengths swing wildly from slice to slice and sit far above the series' true 60-year content, and the strongest slices rival the real climate record. A smoke detector that goes off in every empty room tells you nothing when it goes off in yours. L&S11's supplement tested only the easy direction — if a true cycle exists, can the fit recover it? This experiment tests the direction that matters: when no cycle exists, does the fit stay silent? It does not.
