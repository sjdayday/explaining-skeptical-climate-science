Waves can rebuild anything

Loehle & Scafetta rebuilt the temperature record out of two waves and a line, and presented the good fit as evidence of real 60- and 20-year cycles. But a fact known since Fourier says the rebuild always works — on any wiggly line at all. Test that claim yourself.

The exercise: choose a graph that has nothing to do with climate, or generate a random one. Then move the slider to give the rebuild more and more waves. The amber curve — a straight line plus your waves, the same recipe as the paper's model — matches the target more and more closely. It never fails to. That is the point.

This graph, too, contains a 60-unit wave

The temperature debate centres on a 60-year wave. Every graph on this page has a 60-unit wave of its own — the mathematics guarantees that a wave of every period appears in the inventory, with some nonzero amplitude. Here is this graph's:

Finding a wave of some chosen period, with some fitted strength, is not a discovery — it is bookkeeping. The discovery would be showing the wave had to be there for a physical reason, and that is the part curve fitting cannot supply.
What this exercise shows. The rebuild succeeded on a stock chart, a mountain ridge, twenty centuries of population — and on randomly generated data. It always succeeds, so succeeding carries no information about causes. Writing a melody down in notes does not mean a piano composed it. When L&S11's two waves and a line matched the temperature record, this same guarantee was at work — the good fit reflects a property of the mathematics, not a discovery about nature.

Honest labels. Stock price: synthetic random walk (seeded, reproducible) — real prices would behave the same way. Mountain skyline: schematic fractal ridge. European population: approximate scholarly estimates (McEvedy & Jones 1978; UN), interpolated between sparse historical anchor points and plotted on a logarithmic scale; the dips are the Plague of Justinian and the Black Death. Random graph: seeded noise, new seed each press. Method: subtract a least-squares line, decompose the leftover into Fourier waves, rebuild with the strongest k waves (computed on a mirrored copy of the record, so the waves aren't forced to wrap its ends around to meet each other) — the same “line plus waves” family as L&S11's Eq. (1), with the wave budget on a slider.
