Skeptic papers, examined · Case file 01 · Curve fitting

The 60‑Year Cycle
That Wasn't

How a 2011 paper built the claim that humans cause only half of global warming from two waves and a straight line — and what replication revealed about the method.

The claim, in brief

Global temperature is mostly two natural cycles — 60 and 20 years long, astronomical in origin — riding a gentle natural recovery from the Little Ice Age. Subtract the cycles and the human contribution is a modest ramp, about half of what the IPCC says — at most. Climate sensitivity falls to roughly 1–1.5 °C per CO₂ doubling, and the 21st century should warm by only ~0.6 °C above 2000 — less than 1 °C in total.

The rebuttal, in brief

That is curve fitting, not discovery. Any wiggly record can be rebuilt from waves; 160 years is far too short to establish a 60‑year rhythm; the same method finds equally convincing “cycles” in random data; the proposed physics contradicts itself; and the paper’s forecast has since failed against real temperatures.

PART ONE

The original argument, at its strongest

In July 2011, ecologist Craig Loehle and physicist Nicola Scafetta published “Climate Change Attribution Using Empirical Decomposition of Climatic Data” in The Open Atmospheric Science Journal — a broad open‑access outlet rather than a specialist climate journal. The paper became a fixture of skeptic media because it offered something rare: a simple, checkable model with a concrete forecast.

The intuition

Look at the global temperature record and you can see a slow alternation: warmth around the 1940s, a cool spell around the 1970s, warmth again around 2000. Scafetta’s earlier work argued that rhythms of roughly 60 and 20 years arise from the motion of the giant planets, which subtly shifts the Sun; ocean cycles like the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation seem to keep similar time. And here is the argument’s key premise: before 1950, human influence was too small to matter much — the authors lean on mainstream sources for this, citing attribution work placing the emergence of a dominant human signal around 1950–1960 and noting that earlier greenhouse forcing was largely offset by aerosols. So a model trained only on pre‑1950 data should capture pure nature — and whatever it fails to explain afterwards is the human share.

What they did

Their model is one line of mathematics:

y(t) = A·cos[2π(t−T₁)/60] + B·cos[2π(t−T₂)/20] + C·(t−1900) + D

The periods are pinned at exactly 60 and 20 years — imported from Scafetta’s earlier analysis of the solar system’s motion — while the heights (A, B), timings (T₁, T₂), and the line (C, D) are left for least‑squares fitting to choose. A detail worth noting: the authors mention that letting the data pick the long period freely gives a value close to 60, and their own supplement’s free fit centers near 65 years; they imposed 60 anyway, on astronomical grounds.

They fitted in two stages. Stage one fitted the equation to the entire HadCRUT3 record, 1850–2010 — and judged the result deficient: the leftovers weren’t random, drifting upward late in the record. Rather than discard the model, they read that drift as the fingerprint of post‑war human warming, and refitted using data up to 1950 only. Two things about the refit impressed readers. First, the extended curve stayed in phase with the observations: the 1940s bump, the 1970s dip, the crest around 2000, even the apparent “plateau” of the 2000s. Second, the leftover — observation minus model — now formed a clean straight ramp: fitted over 1951–2010, it rises at 0.66 ± 0.08 °C per century and crosses zero in 1942. That ramp, they argued, is the human contribution: emissions, urbanization, land‑use change.

The supporting evidence

The paper doesn’t rest on the 160‑year fit alone. A full section collects independent reports of a roughly‑60‑year rhythm: a Caribbean seabed record whose best‑fit period is 61.5 ± 4 years; a tree‑ring reconstruction of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation at 59.5 ± 4; a 500‑year Mediterranean temperature reconstruction; ocean fish‑stock cycles; the global sea‑level record; even cosmogenic isotopes in ice. The proposed clock is the solar system itself — Jupiter and Saturn’s alignments repeat on a ~60‑year beat, nudging the Sun, whose activity (they argue, via a solar‑irradiance proxy model of their own construction, plus possible cosmic‑ray effects on clouds) then paces Earth’s climate and also supplies the gentle pre‑1950 warming line.

What they concluded

The accounting, in their telling: about 50 ± 10% of the ~0.8 °C of warming from 1850 to 2010 is natural — Little‑Ice‑Age recovery plus cycles. Of the ~0.5 °C of warming from 1970 to 2000, roughly 0.3 °C is just the two cycles swinging together from their 1970 minima to their 2000 maxima — about 60% of it. The mid‑century cooling needs no sulfate‑aerosol explanation; it was the 60‑year cycle’s downswing, and they note the residuals show no aerosol‑shaped dip. Even the modest human ramp, they add, is an upper limit, since poorly corrected urban‑heat and land‑use contamination may account for part of it (here they cite, among others, McKitrick & Michaels — a paper that sits elsewhere on the same list of 38 this series examines). Climate sensitivity lands near 1–1.5 °C per CO₂ doubling — a third to half of the IPCC’s best estimate, hinting, they say, at a slight negative feedback. And since the cycle peaked around 2000 and now descends, human warming stays masked until about 2040; their model, extended to 2100, tops out near 0.6 °C above 2000 — against the ~3 °C they attribute to IPCC projections. In the abstract’s words, climate “may warm less than 1 °C by 2100.” They also claim early confirmation: the flat 2000s, which IPCC‑class models had not predicted, was already in their curve.

Why it persuaded people. It looked like science at its best: fit the past, predict the future, keep the math simple enough for anyone to check. It seemed to “predict” the 2000s slowdown while mainstream models were being criticized for missing it. And it carried a reassuring bottom line. To see what is wrong with it, you have to look at the machinery — which is what the rebuttal did.
PART TWO

The rebuttal: five ways the machinery fails

Benestad and colleagues re‑ran L&S11’s analysis as part of their 2016 replication study of 38 contrarian papers, filing it under the heading curve‑fitting. Their objections stack up from mathematics to physics. Each objection links to a hands‑on exercise — the “try it yourself” boxes — where you can run the argument yourself, in the browser.

Objection 1

Waves are a language, not a cause

A mathematical fact known since Fourier: any wiggly record — stock prices, your heartbeat, a mountain skyline — can be rebuilt as a sum of waves. The rebuild always succeeds. So succeeding tells you nothing about what caused the wiggles. Writing a melody down in notes doesn’t mean a piano composed it. L&S11’s fit is bookkeeping presented as discovery.

Try it yourself · Exercise 1 Rebuild anything out of waves — a stock chart, a mountain skyline, twenty centuries of European population, or randomly generated data. The rebuild always succeeds.
Objection 2

Two and a half beats don’t make a rhythm

The whole record holds 160 years — fewer than three repeats of a 60‑year cycle, and the calibration window holds fewer than two. You cannot establish a rhythm from two beats. One symptom: other periods fit the same data as well — or better. Benestad et al. found that 65.75‑ and 21.5‑year waves fit HadCRUT3 better than 60 and 20. L&S11’s own paper concedes the point: they note that a free fit lands only “close to” 60, and the free‑fit target in their own supplement centers near 65 years — they imposed 60 anyway to match the astronomy. In the 2013 open review, Loehle added a further concession from the other side: a free fit of the periods gave results “visually almost indistinguishable” from the fixed‑period fit — meaning the record cannot tell 60 from its neighbours, so the fit lends the astronomical clock no independent support. On NOAA’s data we find the best “long cycle” is ~87 years. A constant of nature should not change when the dataset changes, or disagree with its discoverers’ own free fit.

Try it yourself · Exercise 2 Turn the period dial — refit their model live on the real data and find the period that fits best. It is not 60.
Objection 3

The method finds cycles in pure randomness

This is the rebuttal’s centerpiece. Build a 10,000‑year artificial series out of hundreds of waves with random strengths and timings — structured noise with no special 60‑year cause. Slice it into 160‑year pieces, the length of the real record, and run L&S11’s fit on each slice. Every slice “finds” a 60‑year cycle. The apparent strength swings wildly from slice to slice, is inflated far above the series’ true 60‑year content (about four‑fold in Benestad et al.’s version; about an order of magnitude in ours — the exact factor depends on the noise recipe), and the strongest slices rival the real climate record. A smoke detector that goes off in every empty room tells you nothing when it goes off in yours.

Try it yourself · Exercise 3 Slice a random series — draw 160‑year slices of seeded noise, run their exact fit, and count how many times it reports no cycle. (It never does.)
Objection 4

Flexible models must face data they’ve never seen — this one didn’t

With enough adjustable parts, a model fits whatever it’s tuned to; a saying attributed to John von Neumann holds that with four parameters one can fit an elephant. Benestad et al.’s charge is that L&S11 never properly evaluated their model against independent data: the wiggle‑match through 2010 reuses the era that shaped the model’s human ramp, and the pre‑1950 “nature‑only” premise quietly assumes the very conclusion (a small early human effect) the paper set out to prove. The only clean test is data that arrived after publication. Part Three returns to this.

Try it yourself · Exercise 4 Run the overfitting experiment — raise the wave count, fit the past as closely as you like, then reveal 2011–2025 and score the forecast.
Objection 5

The physics contradicts itself

To connect distant planets to Earth’s thermometers, the paper leans on resonance — the climate supposedly rings like a struck glass in sympathy with small astronomical nudges, while responding only sluggishly (a “slight negative feedback”) to the far larger energy push of added CO₂. But resonance is a property of the system, not of the forcing it prefers: a wine glass rings at its pitch for any sound at that pitch, whatever the source. Benestad et al. demonstrate with a simulated oscillator that a resonant system gets excited by noisy or trending pushes of any origin, greenhouse forcing included. A climate sensitive enough to amplify small planetary pushes could not also be insensitive to the much larger push from CO₂. And that exposes the deeper problem — the false dichotomy: even if 20‑ and 60‑year wiggles are real (similar ocean variability appears in climate models too), wiggles ride on top of trends. Finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from CO₂. The paper never tested the greenhouse explanation it claimed to displace. One more structural weakness: the causal chain runs planets → solar activity → irradiance and cosmic‑ray/cloud effects → climate, and nearly every link is borrowed from contested sources — a solar‑irradiance history built on the solar‑cycle‑length idea, the disputed ACRIM satellite composite, and cosmic‑ray cloud seeding. The first and last of those are themselves examined and faulted elsewhere in the same replication study (the Friis‑Christensen & Lassen and Svensmark papers). The chain’s links rest largely on one another rather than on independent physics.

Try it yourself · Exercise 5 Push a resonant system — with a planetary metronome, with random noise, with a greenhouse ramp, or by hand. See whether it responds differently to any of them.
The verdict in the study’s taxonomy. Benestad et al. file L&S11 under logic type B — the machinery is applied, but the premises are wrong — with the shorthand label curve fitting: an over‑fit set of waves, no proper evaluation, implausible physics, and relevant literature ignored. Their analysis is reproducible via the open R package replicationDemos (function LoehleScafetta2011()).
PART THREE

What happened after publication

Every forecast eventually meets the future. L&S11’s model says the 60‑year cycle has been in its downswing since ~2000, holding temperatures nearly flat — our faithful re‑run of their full model rises just 0.05 °C between 2000 and 2030 — on the way to less than 1 °C of warming for the whole century. Here is what the planet did instead.

This is the outcome the rebuttal anticipated: a curve fitted to fluctuations has no reason to keep working once the fluctuations change. The 2000s “plateau” that made the model appear skilful turned out to be exactly the kind of temporary fluctuation the mainstream literature said it was. The arithmetic is simple: the paper’s published ceiling for the entire century was about 0.6 °C of warming above 2000. Comparing decade averages to smooth out El Niño swings, the world warmed 0.47 °C between 1996–2005 and 2016–2025 — roughly three‑quarters of that hundred‑year allowance, spent in the first two decades, with individual recent years already past the ceiling.

SIDE BY SIDE

The two arguments at a glance

The human share? At most a ramp of ≈0.66 °C/century emerging in 1942 — about half the IPCC’s attribution, possibly less if urban‑heat contamination inflates it; sensitivity ~1–1.5 °C per doubling. What would count as proof? The model’s match to 1951–2010. Only genuine out‑of‑sample skill, tested against data the model never touched. It failed that test.
ANTICIPATING THE COMEBACKS

Four defenses, four answers

These defenses are not hypothetical. All four were made in writing during the 2013 public peer review of the rebuttal’s first version — posted to Earth System Dynamics Discussions under the title “Agnotology: learning from mistakes,” debated in the open by the criticized authors and the rebuttal team, and not accepted there before the expanded paper appeared in 2016. The key documents ship with this project in sources/esdd-open-review/; the Going deeper section gives the full record.

“But it predicted the plateau!”

The strongest defense is that the 1951–2010 wiggle‑match was a successful prediction — the paper itself claims the post‑2000 flattening as evidence in hand. Two answers. First, matching the phase of one or two wiggles is weak evidence: the random slices in Exhibit D produce phase‑coherent, convincing‑looking wiggles all the time, and a fit anchored on the 1940s bump and 1970s dip is halfway to “predicting” a 2000s crest by arithmetic alone. Second, the model’s decisive prediction — the flat decades after 2010 — is the one that failed. When a model’s successes are the flexible parts and its failures are the rigid parts, that is the signature of curve fitting.

“But they validated the method in their supplement!”

They did anticipate the too‑short‑record objection. Their Supplemental Information 1 takes the fitted cycles‑plus‑line curve as a true signal, sprinkles white noise on top, refits on 1850–1950 a thousand times, and shows the period is recovered (64.1 ± 2.6 years against a target of 64.9). But notice what that tests: if the world really is two fixed cycles plus a line plus white noise, the fit can find them. It says nothing about the reverse risk — that the fit “finds” cycles when none exist. Real climate noise isn’t white; it’s structured, with slow wandering built in. Feed the same fit structured randomness containing no fixed cycle — Exhibit D — and it reports convincing 60‑year cycles anyway. A validation that assumes the conclusion cannot defend it. (Note also that their own validation target sits near 65 years, not 60.)

“But proxy records show 60‑year cycles for centuries!”

The paper’s supporting studies show quasi‑oscillations in the 50–80‑year range, in particular regions, waxing and waning in strength — which is exactly what slow, structured natural variability looks like. That is not a fixed global clock. In the open review Scafetta pressed this defense, insisting the quasi‑60‑year pattern is “evident at naked eyes to any unbiased person” and pointing to the paper’s Figure 4 proxies. Taking those two records on their own terms: best‑fit periods of 61.5 ± 4 and 59.5 ± 4 years, five swings each since 1650, disagreeing with each other and with 60 — five noisy repeats with ±4‑year slack is what broadband ocean variability produces, not a phase‑locked planetary clock. (Exhibit D also answers the appeal to the eye: its slices of cause‑free noise look just as convincing.) The wider contrarian cycle literature, surveyed in Benestad et al.’s Table S2, reports “dominant” periods from 6 to 2,804 years — claims that cannot all be true and are rarely reconciled with one another. And the fundamental answer stands: ocean oscillations of similar size appear in standard climate models as internal variability, no planets required — and even a genuine quasi‑cycle would ride on top of the greenhouse trend, subtracting nothing from it.

“But the cycles were a hypothesis — and the noise test didn’t copy our method!”

Loehle’s written defense makes two further claims. First, that the 60‑ and 20‑year periods were never found in the 160‑year record at all — they were imported as a hypothesis from Scafetta’s solar‑system analysis, with only the heights and timings left to fit, so the too‑few‑beats objection attacks a step the paper never took. Second, that the rebuttal’s synthetic‑data demonstration “did not replicate our method.” Exhibit D answers both at once, because it grants the hypothesis framing entirely: each slice of noise is fitted exactly as Eq. (1) fits the climate record — periods pinned at 60 and 20 in advance, heights and timings free. That is the imported‑hypothesis procedure, run on data known to contain no such clock, and it reports convincing cycles anyway. Importing the period does not remove the flexibility that matters — two free timings, two free heights, and a line; it relocates the burden of proof onto the astronomy, the contested chain of Objection 5. And a hypothesis earns support only if the data fit it better than cause‑free noise would; the slices show they do not.

Benestad’s reply in the same thread stated the core point plainly: the weakness is not the exact fitting recipe, nor whether the true period is 60 or 70 years — with only about two repeats of noisy geophysical data, no degree of in‑sample fit can confer physical significance. He closed with a sentence worth preserving for the spirit of this series: “It would indeed be very good news [if] Loehle and Scafetta (2011) were right.”

IN BRIEF

The argument in five sentences

  • Any wiggly line can be rebuilt out of waves — the rebuild always works, so it proves nothing about causes.
  • 160 years contains two and a half “60‑year cycles.” A rhythm cannot be established from two and a half beats.
  • Run their exact method on random noise and it finds 60‑year cycles there too — often just as strong.
  • A climate that amplifies small planetary pushes cannot be insensitive to the larger push from CO₂ — resonance does not select among causes.
  • Their ceiling for the whole century was about 0.6 °C above 2000 — and the first two decades used three‑quarters of it.

Station 1 · §2, Decomposition Analysis · p. 5

The mechanism is acknowledged as speculation

“The physical mechanisms responsible for the cycles are not understood yet and, therefore, are not included in the current climate models. However, it is reasonable to speculate that planetary tides and solar angular motion affect solar activity [Scafetta, 2010; Wolff and Patrone, 2010]. Changes of solar activity then influence the Earth's climate through changes of direct total solar irradiance, the sun's magnetic field and the solar wind. Very likely, solar activity changes influence also the intensity of cosmic rays reaching the Earth … The latter influences the generation of clouds which modulate terrestrial albedo and, consequently, the climate.” L&S11, §2, preprint p. 5

The causal chain — planets → solar activity → irradiance and cosmic rays/clouds → climate — is introduced here as a speculation, before any fitting is done. The rebuttal examines both the chain's internal logic (a resonant climate would respond to any matching push, not only this one) and its links, several of which are faulted elsewhere in the same replication study.

Station 2 · §2 · p. 6

The dividing line at 1950 is assumed

“A key to the analysis is the assumption that anthropogenic forcings may be the predominant forcing during the second half of the 20th century with a net forcing of about 1.6 W/m² since 1950 [e.g., Hegerl et al., 2001; Thompson et al., 2009]. … Before about 1950 (although we estimate a more precise date) the climate effect of elevated greenhouse gases was no doubt small [IPCC, 2007].” L&S11, §2, preprint p. 6

Everything downstream depends on this line: the model is calibrated on the pre‑1950 years as if they were purely natural, and whatever it later fails to explain is, by construction, labelled anthropogenic. The rebuttal's point is not that the 1950 date is indefensible — the paper cites mainstream sources for it — but that treating the pre‑1950 fit as “nature captured” assumes the small early human effect the paper set out to establish.

Station 3 · §2 · pp. 6–7

The periods are imported, and the rest is left free

“Given the strong periodicity in the solar signal detected by Scafetta [2010], we used this model, with two cycles of lengths 60 and 20 years, plus a linear term. A free fit of the Hadley HadCRUT3 global surface temperature data found cycles quite close to this, but we use the Scafetta solar periodicities plus a linear trend. … The model was fit by nonlinear least squares estimation using Mathematica functions, with phase and amplitude free but period fixed as above.” L&S11, §2, preprint pp. 6–7

Two method choices happen here. First, the model family: waves plus a line, which can match any wiggly record given free amplitudes and phases — so a good match carries no information about causes. Second, the periods: fixed at 60 and 20 on astronomical grounds, with the paper noting that the data's own free fit lands only “quite close to” them (the supplement's free‑fit target is ≈65 years, and other periods fit the calibration window as well or better).

Station 4 · §2 · p. 7

The failed full fit becomes the refit to 1950

“When fit to the entire 160 year period, the model … performs only decently (Fig. 1a). However, the nonrandom residual signal over the entire period (Fig. 1b) reveals that the model is deficient. Since this mis-specification could result from not including anthropogenic effects that could have accelerated the warming since 1950, the model was refit using the data only up to 1950 (Fig. 2a).” L&S11, §2, preprint p. 7

This is the paper's pivotal move. The full‑record fit fails; instead of reading the failure as evidence against the cycles‑plus‑line model, the authors attribute it to a missing human term and refit on the shorter window. Alternative readings — a different period would fit better, or no fixed cycle exists at all — are not tested at this point. The two figures below are the direct product of this choice.

Station 5 · §2 · p. 8

The residual becomes the human share, and chance is ruled out

“A linear fit to the residuals since 1950 (Fig. 3) has a slope of 0.66 +/- 0.08 °C/century (R² = 0.59) and begins (>0) in 1942. … It is very unlikely that the 60 and 20-year cycles would match the pattern of temperature over this period by chance; thus, the empirical model calibrated on the temperature data before 1950 has the capability of forecasting the multidecadal oscillations of the temperature after 1942.” L&S11, §2, preprint p. 8

Two claims are made: the leftover ramp is the human contribution, and the wiggle‑match after 1950 is too good to be chance — therefore the model can forecast. The second claim is testable, and the rebuttal tests it: 160‑year slices of structured randomness, fitted the same way, produce equally convincing matches routinely.

The validation assumes the cycle it defends

“We take the model from Fig. 1 as the target. A sample set is obtained by randomly adding white noise to this model with variance derived from Fig. 1b (sd = 0.13 °C). … Repeating this 1000 times, the mean estimated period is 64.11 yr vs. 64.93 for the target, with sd = 2.63 yr. PLAIN WORDS

Glossary

Curve fitting / overfitting
Adjusting a flexible formula until it closely fits past data. The more adjustable parts used to achieve the fit, the less the fit says about the future.
Sinusoid (wave, harmonic)
The smooth, endlessly repeating up‑and‑down curve of a single frequency. Fourier’s theorem: stack enough of them and you can reproduce any finite record exactly.
Residual
What’s left of the data after subtracting a model. L&S11’s entire human‑contribution estimate is a straight line drawn through a residual.
Out‑of‑sample test
Checking a model against data it never saw during tuning. The only reliable test of a flexible model — and the one that began, in this case, after publication in 2011.
R² (“R‑squared”)
A 0‑to‑1 score for how much of the data’s variation a model accounts for. High R² on the data used for fitting is easy to achieve; high R² on unseen data is the meaningful test.
Resonance
A system’s tendency to respond strongly to pushes near its natural frequency — like a playground swing. It responds to any push at that tempo, whatever the source.
False dichotomy
“It’s natural cycles, therefore it isn’t CO₂.” Wiggles and trends coexist; demonstrating one does not subtract the other.
Attribution
The science of assigning causes to observed climate change — properly done with physics and multiple independent lines of evidence, rather than by labeling a residual.

This case file re-runs everything on NOAA's NOAAGlobalTemp, fetched via the open datasets/global-temp mirror. That the “discovered” best period moves between these records is itself part of the argument (Objection 2).
Where “60 and 20 years” come from
The periods are not fitted — they are imported from planetary astronomy. Jupiter and Saturn realign about every 19.86 years (the great conjunction), and three such beats return the conjunction to nearly the same region of sky in ≈60 years. Scafetta (2010) argued that global temperature shares spectral peaks near those periods with the Sun's wobble around the solar system's centre of mass, and that ocean rhythms like the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation keep similar time. L&S11 adopt 60 and 20 on that authority; everything else in Eq. (1) — heights, timings, line — is least-squares bookkeeping. Whether the data independently prefer those periods is exactly what Objection 2 and Exercise 2 test; whether the proposed physical link could work is Objection 5.

The primary documents

The claim
Loehle & Scafetta (2011), “Climate Change Attribution Using Empirical Decomposition of Climatic Data,” The Open Atmospheric Science Journal: published article · arXiv preprint — the version this project ships in its sources/ folder.
The rebuttal
Benestad et al. (2016), “Learning from mistakes in climate research,” Theoretical and Applied Climatology: open-access article. Its supporting material — the “Curve-fitting” section this case file examines, plus Table S2 — downloads from the same page; page archives of both ship in sources/.
The rebuttal's code
replicationDemos, Benestad's open R package; its function LoehleScafetta2011() re-runs the analysis behind the “Curve-fitting” section.
The adversarial record
The rebuttal's first version, titled “Agnotology: learning from mistakes,” went through public peer review at Earth System Dynamics Discussions (2013), where the criticized authors filed defenses in their own words — Loehle (C253), Scafetta (C318, C312), Solheim, Humlum, Stordahl & Yndestad (C257), McKitrick (C261) — Benestad replied (C258, C269, C340), and the editor ultimately declined the revision (C400); the reorganized and expanded paper appeared in Theor. Appl. Climatol. in 2016. Every comment PDF remains freely available on the discussion page; extracted texts of the L&S11 thread ship in sources/esdd-open-review/. The “Four defenses, four answers” section answers the defenses made there.

Methods & honesty notes

This draft has been checked line‑by‑line against the original preprint (arXiv:1206.5845, now in the project files). Exhibits B, C and E re‑run the L&S11 procedure — their Eq. (1), periods fixed at 60 and 20 years, amplitudes and phases free, linear term, calibration 1850–1950 — on NOAA’s NOAAGlobalTemp annual anomalies, 1850–2025, relative to the 20th‑century average. Our ordinary‑least‑squares sine/cosine formulation is algebraically equivalent to their fixed‑period cosine fit. Dataset vintage explains the numerical differences: our 60‑yr amplitude 0.100 °C vs. their 0.121 ± 0.016; 20‑yr 0.043 vs. 0.041 ± 0.016; cycle peaks ≈1997/1998 vs. their 1998.6/1999.7; calibration R² 0.46 vs. 0.53; residual ramp 0.81 °C/century crossing ~1947 vs. their 0.66 ± 0.08 (fitted 1950–2010) crossing 1942. Exhibit D reproduces the supporting material’s synthetic experiment: a 10,000‑year sum of ~400 waves with random strengths and timings, scaled to the real record’s detrended variability, sliced into 62 pieces of 160 years; the inflation factor (ours ~14×, theirs ~4×) depends on the noise recipe — the instability is the finding, not the exact factor. This differs deliberately from the paper’s own Supplemental test, which adds white noise to an assumed‑true cycle and therefore only measures recovery‑given‑truth, not the false‑alarm rate. All figures were generated for this project; the script, template and data ship alongside this document so every number can be rebuilt — in the same spirit of open replication the rebuttal practised.
