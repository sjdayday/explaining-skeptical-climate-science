Resonance: a short primer

Objection 5 turns on a word that L&S11 never use. The rebuttal reads their picture of the climate — strongly amplifying small astronomical pushes, weakly answering the much larger push from CO₂ — as describing a resonant system, then works out what that description requires. This page explains what a resonant system is, gives familiar examples, and spells out the argument's structure.

What makes a system resonant

The recipe has three ingredients: something that can swing back and forth between two forms of energy (a restoring force plus inertia), and only weak losses per cycle. Weak damping means the system “remembers” pushes long enough for well-timed ones to accumulate — that memory is what makes it ring at its own natural frequency. Push it at that tempo and small efforts build a large swing; push it off-tempo and the same efforts cancel each other.

Examples

Mechanical.

  • A playground swing — the case file's glossary example. A child pumping, or a parent giving small shoves at the right moments, builds a big arc; the same effort applied off-tempo goes nowhere.
  • A wine glass — Objection 5's example. It rings at its pitch when struck, and a singer holding that pitch loudly enough can shatter it: a faint-but-tuned push outperforming a strong-but-untuned one, which is exactly the intuition L&S11's picture relies on.
  • Strings, tuning forks, bells — struck once (an impulse), each responds at its own frequencies, regardless of how it was struck.
  • Buildings and bridges. Skyscrapers sway with natural periods of several seconds, which is why earthquake engineering matches ground-shaking periods against building heights: in the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, soft lakebed soil amplified waves near the sway period of mid-rise buildings, which failed disproportionately. London's Millennium Bridge (2000) swayed because pedestrians unconsciously synchronized their steps to its sideways mode — the walkers became the metronome.
  • A car's suspension bouncing at its own rate over evenly spaced potholes.

Acoustic.

  • Blowing across a bottle's mouth: your breath is broadband noise — structureless, like Exercise 5's “random weather” — yet the bottle answers with one clear pitch. This is the best everyday analog of the noise scenario: no rhythm in, rhythm out.
  • Organ pipes, flutes, the body of a violin, a shower stall that flatters your singing at certain notes.

Electrical.

  • A radio's tuning circuit: tuning is resonance — the circuit responds strongly to one broadcast frequency out of the electromagnetic din. Note that it picks a frequency, never a station's identity: two transmitters on the same frequency both get through. That is the frequency-not-source point in another costume.
  • Quartz crystals in watches and computers, ringing at extremely stable frequencies with very weak damping.
  • MRI machines — nuclear magnetic resonance, literally in the name: nuclei respond when the radio pulse matches their precession frequency.

Natural, at geophysical scale.

  • Tides in certain basins: the Bay of Fundy's natural sloshing period (~13 hours) sits close to the ~12.4-hour lunar tidal forcing, which is why its tides reach roughly 15 metres while the open ocean's are under one.
  • Seiches — a sloshing bathtub or harbor: one shove and the water rocks at the basin's own period.
  • Orbital resonances: Jupiter's moons Io, Europa and Ganymede locked in 1:2:4 orbits; gaps in the asteroid belt swept clean where orbital periods resonate with Jupiter's.

How a resonant system answers different pushes

A resonant system responds to any forcing — but the character of the response depends on the forcing's frequency content, not on its origin:

A periodic push near the natural period is amplified: large swings from a small push. This is the only regime in which resonance discriminates — and it discriminates by tempo, never by identity of the pusher.
A broadband push — noise, or a one-off impulse contains some energy near the natural period, and the system selectively amplifies that part. The response oscillates at the system's own period even though the push had no such rhythm. (A volcanic eruption is a real-world impulse of this kind; Exercise 5's “Push now” button is another.)
A slow trend — such as greenhouse forcing — is transmitted: the system tracks it quasi-statically. Not boosted like a resonant beat, but not rejected either.

The one-sentence summary: resonance is selective by frequency, never by source. What L&S11's accounting requires is a system selective by source — one that amplifies the astronomical push while effectively rejecting the much larger CO₂ push. No passive resonant system does that. And there is a second edge: to amplify a tiny push into the observed swings at all, a system needs very weak damping or a positive feedback — and any such amplification machinery would be equally available to greenhouse forcing.

The structure of Objection 5

With that background, the objection is a translation followed by a conditional argument. L&S11 never say “resonance”; they describe a climate in which tiny planetary influences arrive pre-amplified while CO₂ meets a “slight negative feedback.” Any system matching that description just is a frequency-selective amplifier — a resonant system, in standard physics vocabulary. That is what the supporting material means by “L&S11 assumed some kind of selective and potent resonance”: a reconstruction from the paper's numbers, not a quotation from its text. The argument then runs as a dilemma, granting their picture for the sake of argument:

  • If the climate is resonant enough at ~60 years to amplify planetary whispers, then (a) ordinary internal noise also rings it at 60 years — so the observed wiggle cannot identify the planets as the cause, and the attribution fails; and (b) the CO₂ trend passes through — so the selective deafness their accounting needs is physically unavailable.
  • If the climate is not such a resonator, the proposed mechanism has no way to turn tiny astronomical nudges into the observed swings in the first place.

Either branch defeats the attribution. Note what the argument does not claim: neither Benestad et al. nor this case file asserts that the real climate is a 60-year resonator. Every example above has an identifiable oscillator with a physical restoring mechanism — a string's tension, a basin's geometry, a circuit's inductance — and L&S11 never identify what the climate's 60-year “string” would be. A sharp 60-year resonance would also require the climate to ring with little loss for centuries: very weak damping in a turbulent, dissipative system, which the supporting material calls unlikely. Real ocean modes are more like a heavily damped slosh, giving broad, wandering quasi-periodicities — “50 to 80 years, sometimes,” not “60.0, always” — which is the bridge back to Objection 2: a record like that is what broadband variability looks like, no metronome required.

The four buttons of Exercise 5 map onto this argument one-to-one. Metronome = the claim's proposed cause (works, as granted). Random weather = internal variability (also works — attribution lost).
