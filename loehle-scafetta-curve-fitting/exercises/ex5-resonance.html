Resonance doesn't pick sides

To connect distant planets to Earth's thermometers, L&S11 lean on resonance: the climate rings like a struck glass in sympathy with tiny astronomical nudges — while supposedly responding only sluggishly to the far larger push of CO₂. But resonance is a property of the system, not of the pusher it prefers. Here is a resonant system. Push it yourself.

The game: below is a simulated oscillator with a natural period of 60 years and a gentle ring — a stand-in for the claim's “resonant climate,” the same demonstration Benestad et al. ran in their supporting material. You choose what pushes it. The claim's story needs the first button to work and the others to fail. See what actually happens.

What this exercise shows. The metronome works — a whisper at the right tempo builds a big swing. But so does everything else. Random, rhythm-free shoves excite the same 60-year ring (a lesson for Exercise 3: a 60-year wobble in a record needs no 60-year cause). And the greenhouse ramp passes straight through: the system rides the rising push while it wobbles — trends and wiggles coexist, so finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from CO₂. A wine glass rings at its pitch for the soprano, the cello, and the crowd alike. A climate sensitive enough to amplify planetary whispers cannot also be deaf to CO₂'s shout — yet that double standard is precisely what L&S11's accounting requires. And even this concedes too much: unlike your finger, the planetary “whisper” has no accepted physical mechanism to push with in the first place — the links in their chain (irradiance reconstructions, cosmic-ray cloud seeding) are themselves faulted elsewhere in the same replication study.

You have now run all five objections with your own hands: waves rebuild anything (Exercise 1); a 100-year window can't certify a 60-year rhythm (Exercise 2); the fit “discovers” cycles in dice rolls (Exercise 3); memorizing the past predicts nothing (Exercise 4); and the proposed physics can't pick sides (Exercise 5).
