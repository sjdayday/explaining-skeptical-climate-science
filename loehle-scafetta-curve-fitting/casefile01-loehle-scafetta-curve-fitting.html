The 60‑Year Cycle
That Wasn't

How a 2011 paper turned two waves and a straight line into the claim that humans cause only half of global warming — and how replication took the argument apart.

The claim, in one breath

Global temperature is mostly two natural cycles — 60 and 20 years long, astronomical in origin — riding a gentle natural recovery from the Little Ice Age. Subtract the cycles and the human contribution is a modest ramp, about half of what the IPCC says. The 21st century should warm by less than 1 °C.

The rebuttal, in one breath

That is curve fitting, not discovery. Any wiggly record can be rebuilt from waves; 160 years is far too short to establish a 60‑year rhythm; the same method finds equally convincing “cycles” in pure randomness; the proposed physics contradicts itself; and the paper’s forecast has since failed against real temperatures.

PART ONE

The original argument, given its best shot

In July 2011, ecologist Craig Loehle and physicist Nicola Scafetta published “Climate Change Attribution Using Empirical Decomposition of Climatic Data” in The Open Atmospheric Science Journal — a broad open‑access outlet rather than a specialist climate journal. The paper became a fixture of skeptic media because it offered something rare: a simple, checkable model with a concrete forecast.

The intuition

Look at the global temperature record and you can see a slow heartbeat: warmth around the 1940s, a cool spell around the 1970s, warmth again around 2000. Scafetta’s earlier work argued that rhythms of roughly 60 and 20 years arise from the motion of the giant planets, which subtly shifts the Sun; ocean cycles like the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation seem to keep similar time. And here is the argument’s engine: before 1950, human emissions were too small to matter much. So a model trained only on pre‑1950 data should capture pure nature — and whatever it fails to explain afterwards is the human share.

What they did

They fitted this recipe to the HadCRUT3 global temperature series for 1850–1950 by least squares, then let the fitted curve run forward through 2010. Two things impressed readers. First, the extended curve kept time with reality: the 1940s bump, the 1970s dip, the crest around 2000, even the apparent “plateau” of the 2000s. Second, the leftover — observation minus model — formed a clean straight ramp beginning around 1942, rising at 0.66 ± 0.08 °C per century. That ramp, they said, is us: emissions, urbanization, land‑use change.

What they concluded

If the cycles are natural and the ramp is the human share, then the human contribution since 1970 is roughly half of what the IPCC attributes. The mid‑century cooling needs no sulfate‑aerosol explanation — it was just the 60‑year cycle’s downswing. And since the cycle peaked around 2000 and is now declining, it should offset the modest human ramp for decades. Their forecast, in the abstract’s words, was that climate “may warm less than 1 °C by 2100.”

Why it persuaded people. It looked like science at its best: fit the past, predict the future, keep the math simple enough for anyone to check. It seemed to “predict” the 2000s slowdown while mainstream models were being mocked for missing it. And it carried a comforting bottom line. To see what’s wrong with it, you have to look at the machinery — which is exactly what the rebuttal did.
PART TWO

The rebuttal: five ways the machinery fails

Benestad and colleagues re‑ran L&S11’s analysis as part of their 2016 replication study of 38 contrarian papers, filing it under the heading curve‑fitting. Their objections stack up from mathematics to physics.

Objection 1

Waves are a language, not a cause

A mathematical fact known since Fourier: any wiggly record — stock prices, your heartbeat, a mountain skyline — can be rebuilt as a sum of waves. The rebuild always succeeds. So succeeding tells you nothing about what caused the wiggles. Writing a melody down in notes doesn’t mean a piano composed it. L&S11’s fit is bookkeeping presented as discovery.

Objection 2

Two and a half beats don’t make a rhythm

The whole record holds 160 years — fewer than three repeats of a 60‑year cycle, and the calibration window holds fewer than two. You cannot establish a rhythm from two beats. The tell: other periods fit the same data just as well. Benestad et al. found that 65.75‑ and 21.5‑year waves fit HadCRUT3 better than 60 and 20. On NOAA’s data we find the best “long cycle” is ~87 years. When the discovered constant of nature changes every time you change the dataset, you haven’t discovered a constant of nature.

Objection 3

The method finds cycles in pure randomness

This is the rebuttal’s centerpiece. Build a 10,000‑year artificial series out of hundreds of waves with random strengths and timings — structured noise with no special 60‑year cause. Slice it into 160‑year pieces, the length of the real record, and run L&S11’s fit on each slice. Every slice “finds” a 60‑year cycle. The apparent strength swings wildly from slice to slice, is inflated far above the series’ true 60‑year content (about four‑fold in Benestad et al.’s version; about an order of magnitude in ours — the exact factor depends on the noise recipe), and the strongest slices rival the real climate record. A smoke detector that goes off in every empty room tells you nothing when it goes off in yours.

Objection 4

Flexible models must face data they’ve never seen — this one didn’t

With enough adjustable parts, a model fits whatever it’s tuned to; the physicists’ joke, attributed to John von Neumann, is that with four parameters you can fit an elephant. Benestad et al.’s charge is that L&S11 never properly evaluated their model against independent data: the wiggle‑match through 2010 reuses the era that shaped the model’s human ramp, and the pre‑1950 “nature‑only” premise quietly assumes the very conclusion (a small early human effect) the paper set out to prove. The only clean test is data that arrived after publication. Hold that thought for Part Three.

Objection 5

The physics contradicts itself

To connect distant planets to Earth’s thermometers, the paper leans on resonance — the climate supposedly rings like a struck glass in sympathy with tiny astronomical nudges, while responding only sluggishly (a “slight negative feedback”) to the far larger energy push of added CO₂. But resonance is a property of the system, not of the forcing it prefers: a wine glass rings at its pitch for any sound at that pitch — the soprano, the cello, the crowd. Benestad et al. demonstrate with a simulated oscillator that a resonant system gets excited by noisy or trending pushes of any origin, greenhouse forcing included. A climate sensitive enough to amplify planetary whispers could not also be deaf to CO₂’s shout. And that exposes the deepest flaw — the false dichotomy: even if 20‑ and 60‑year wiggles are real (similar ocean variability appears in climate models too), wiggles ride on top of trends. Finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from CO₂. The paper never tested the greenhouse explanation it claimed to displace.

The verdict in the study’s taxonomy. Benestad et al. file L&S11 under logic type B — the machinery is applied, but the premises are wrong — with the shorthand label curve fitting: an over‑fit set of waves, no proper evaluation, implausible physics, and relevant literature ignored. Their analysis is reproducible via the open R package replicationDemos (function LoehleScafetta2011()).
PART THREE

The test nature ran anyway

Every forecast eventually meets the future. L&S11’s model says the 60‑year cycle has been in its downswing since ~2000, holding temperatures nearly flat — our faithful re‑run of their full model rises just 0.05 °C between 2000 and 2030 — on the way to less than 1 °C of warming for the whole century. Here is what the planet did instead.

This is the punchline the rebuttal predicted in advance: a curve fitted to wiggles has no reason to keep working once the wiggles stop cooperating. The 2000s “plateau” that made the model look prophetic turned out to be exactly the kind of temporary fluctuation the mainstream literature said it was.

SIDE BY SIDE

The two arguments at a glance

Loehle & Scafetta 2011Benestad et al. 2016
What are the wiggles? Real 60‑ & 20‑year cycles of astronomical origin. What wave‑fitting always produces; other periods fit as well or better, and random data yields the same “cycles.”
The human share? A modest ramp, ≈0.66 °C/century since 1942 — about half the IPCC’s attribution. An artifact of assuming pre‑1950 warming was natural and calling the leftover “human” — the premise smuggles in the conclusion.
The physics? Climate resonates with planetary/solar rhythms; CO₂ meets a slight negative feedback. Self‑contradictory: a resonant system amplifies any matching or trending push, greenhouse forcing included.
The forecast? Cycle downswing ⇒ near‑flat decades; under 1 °C of warming by 2100. Curve fits have no predictive warrant — and observations since 2011 have overshot the model by ~0.3–0.7 °C already.
What would count as proof? The model’s match to 1951–2010. Only genuine out‑of‑sample skill, tested against data the model never touched. It failed that test.
ANTICIPATING THE COMEBACK

“But it predicted the plateau!”

The strongest defense of L&S11 is that its 1951–2010 wiggle‑match was a successful prediction. Two answers. First, matching the phase of one or two wiggles is weak evidence: the random slices in Exhibit D produce phase‑coherent, convincing‑looking wiggles all the time, and a fit anchored on the 1940s bump and 1970s dip is halfway to “predicting” a 2000s crest by arithmetic alone. Second, the model’s decisive, no‑excuses prediction — the flat decades after 2010 — is the one that failed. When a model’s successes are the flexible parts and its failures are the rigid parts, that is the signature of curve fitting.

FOR CONVERSATIONS

Five lines that carry the argument

  • Any wiggly line can be rebuilt out of waves — the rebuild always works, so it proves nothing about causes.
  • 160 years contains two and a half “60‑year cycles.” You can’t establish a rhythm from two and a half beats.
  • Run their exact method on random noise and it finds 60‑year cycles there too — often just as strong.
  • A climate that resonates to planetary whispers can’t be deaf to CO₂’s shout — resonance doesn’t pick sides.
  • The paper budgeted under 1 °C of warming for this century; the planet spent most of it in the first decade.
PLAIN WORDS

Glossary

Curve fitting / overfitting
Tuning a flexible formula until it hugs past data. The tighter the hug with the more knobs, the less it says about the future — it has memorized, not understood.
Sinusoid (wave, harmonic)
The smooth, endlessly repeating up‑and‑down curve of pure rhythm. Fourier’s theorem: stack enough of them and you can reproduce any finite record exactly.
Residual
What’s left of the data after subtracting a model. L&S11’s entire human‑contribution estimate is a straight line drawn through a residual.
Out‑of‑sample test
Checking a model against data it never saw during tuning. The only honest exam for a flexible model — and the one nature administered after 2011.
R² (“R‑squared”)
A 0‑to‑1 score for how much of the data’s variation a model soaks up. High R² on the training data is cheap; high R² on unseen data is the real currency.
Resonance
A system’s tendency to respond strongly to pushes near its natural frequency — like a playground swing. It responds to any push at that tempo, whoever is pushing.
False dichotomy
“It’s natural cycles, therefore it isn’t CO₂.” Wiggles and trends coexist; demonstrating one does not subtract the other.
Attribution
The science of assigning causes to observed climate change — properly done with physics and multiple independent lines of evidence, not by labeling leftovers.

Methods & honesty notes

Exhibits B, C and E re‑run the L&S11 procedure (least squares; fixed 60‑ and 20‑year periods; free amplitudes and phases; linear term; calibration 1850–1950) on NOAA’s NOAAGlobalTemp annual anomalies, 1850–2025, relative to the 20th‑century average — a different, more current dataset than the paper’s HadCRUT3, which is why our numbers differ slightly (our residual ramp ≈0.81 °C/century from ~1947 vs. their 0.66 from 1942; our 60‑year amplitude 0.10 °C). Exhibit D reproduces the supporting material’s synthetic experiment: a 10,000‑year sum of ~400 waves with random strengths and timings, scaled to the real record’s detrended variability, sliced into 62 pieces of 160 years; the inflation factor (ours ~14×, theirs ~4×) depends on the noise recipe — the instability is the finding, not the exact factor. All figures were generated for this project; the Python script is included alongside this document so every number can be checked — in the same spirit of open replication the rebuttal championed.
