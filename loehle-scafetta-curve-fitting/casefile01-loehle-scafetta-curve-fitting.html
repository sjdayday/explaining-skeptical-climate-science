Skeptic papers under the microscope · Case file 01 · Curve fitting

The 60‑Year Cycle
That Wasn't

How a 2011 paper turned two waves and a straight line into the claim that humans cause only half of global warming — and how replication took the argument apart.

The claim, in one breath

Global temperature is mostly two natural cycles — 60 and 20 years long, astronomical in origin — riding a gentle natural recovery from the Little Ice Age. Subtract the cycles and the human contribution is a modest ramp, about half of what the IPCC says — at most. Climate sensitivity falls to roughly 1–1.5 °C per CO₂ doubling, and the 21st century should warm by only ~0.6 °C above 2000 — less than 1 °C in total.

The rebuttal, in one breath

That is curve fitting, not discovery. Any wiggly record can be rebuilt from waves; 160 years is far too short to establish a 60‑year rhythm; the same method finds equally convincing “cycles” in pure randomness; the proposed physics contradicts itself; and the paper’s forecast has since failed against real temperatures.

PART ONE

The original argument, given its best shot

In July 2011, ecologist Craig Loehle and physicist Nicola Scafetta published “Climate Change Attribution Using Empirical Decomposition of Climatic Data” in The Open Atmospheric Science Journal — a broad open‑access outlet rather than a specialist climate journal. The paper became a fixture of skeptic media because it offered something rare: a simple, checkable model with a concrete forecast.

The intuition

Look at the global temperature record and you can see a slow heartbeat: warmth around the 1940s, a cool spell around the 1970s, warmth again around 2000. Scafetta’s earlier work argued that rhythms of roughly 60 and 20 years arise from the motion of the giant planets, which subtly shifts the Sun; ocean cycles like the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation seem to keep similar time. And here is the argument’s engine: before 1950, human influence was too small to matter much — the authors lean on mainstream sources for this, citing attribution work placing the emergence of a dominant human signal around 1950–1960 and noting that earlier greenhouse forcing was largely offset by aerosols. So a model trained only on pre‑1950 data should capture pure nature — and whatever it fails to explain afterwards is the human share.

What they did

Their model is one line of mathematics:

y(t) = A·cos[2π(t−T₁)/60] + B·cos[2π(t−T₂)/20] + C·(t−1900) + D

The periods are pinned at exactly 60 and 20 years — imported from Scafetta’s earlier analysis of the solar system’s motion — while the heights (A, B), timings (T₁, T₂), and the line (C, D) are left for least‑squares fitting to choose. A detail worth filing away: the authors mention that letting the data pick the long period freely gives a value close to 60, and their own supplement’s free fit centers near 65 years; they imposed 60 anyway, on astronomical grounds.

They fitted in two stages. Stage one fitted the equation to the entire HadCRUT3 record, 1850–2010 — and judged the result deficient: the leftovers weren’t random, drifting upward late in the record. Rather than discard the model, they read that drift as the fingerprint of post‑war human warming, and refitted using data up to 1950 only. Two things about the refit impressed readers. First, the extended curve kept time with reality: the 1940s bump, the 1970s dip, the crest around 2000, even the apparent “plateau” of the 2000s. Second, the leftover — observation minus model — now formed a clean straight ramp: fitted over 1951–2010, it rises at 0.66 ± 0.08 °C per century and crosses zero in 1942. That ramp, they said, is us: emissions, urbanization, land‑use change.

The supporting cast

The paper doesn’t rest on the 160‑year fit alone. A full section marshals independent sightings of a roughly‑60‑year rhythm: a Caribbean seabed record whose best‑fit period is 61.5 ± 4 years; a tree‑ring reconstruction of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation at 59.5 ± 4; a 500‑year Mediterranean temperature reconstruction; ocean fish‑stock cycles; the global sea‑level record; even cosmogenic isotopes in ice. The proposed clock is the solar system itself — Jupiter and Saturn’s alignments repeat on a ~60‑year beat, nudging the Sun, whose activity (they argue, via a solar‑irradiance proxy model of their own construction, plus possible cosmic‑ray effects on clouds) then paces Earth’s climate and also supplies the gentle pre‑1950 warming line.

What they concluded

The accounting, in their telling: about 50 ± 10% of the ~0.8 °C of warming from 1850 to 2010 is natural — Little‑Ice‑Age recovery plus cycles. Of the ~0.5 °C warming from 1970 to 2000 that alarmed the world, roughly 0.3 °C is just the two cycles swinging together from their 1970 minima to their 2000 maxima — about 60% of it. The mid‑century cooling needs no sulfate‑aerosol explanation; it was the 60‑year cycle’s downswing, and they note the residuals show no aerosol‑shaped dip. Even the modest human ramp, they add, is an upper limit, since poorly corrected urban‑heat and land‑use contamination may account for part of it (here they cite, among others, McKitrick & Michaels — a paper that sits elsewhere on the same list of 38 this series examines). Climate sensitivity lands near 1–1.5 °C per CO₂ doubling — a third to half of the IPCC’s best estimate, hinting, they say, at a slight negative feedback. And since the cycle peaked around 2000 and now descends, human warming stays masked until about 2040; their model, extended to 2100, tops out near 0.6 °C above 2000 — against the ~3 °C they attribute to IPCC projections. In the abstract’s words, climate “may warm less than 1 °C by 2100.” They even claim the first receipts: the flat 2000s, which IPCC‑class models hadn’t predicted, was already in their curve.

Why it persuaded people. It looked like science at its best: fit the past, predict the future, keep the math simple enough for anyone to check. It seemed to “predict” the 2000s slowdown while mainstream models were being mocked for missing it. And it carried a comforting bottom line. To see what’s wrong with it, you have to look at the machinery — which is exactly what the rebuttal did.
PART TWO

The rebuttal: five ways the machinery fails

Benestad and colleagues re‑ran L&S11’s analysis as part of their 2016 replication study of 38 contrarian papers, filing it under the heading curve‑fitting. Their objections stack up from mathematics to physics. Each objection links to a hands‑on exercise — the “try it yourself” boxes — where you can run the argument yourself, in the browser.

Objection 1

Waves are a language, not a cause

A mathematical fact known since Fourier: any wiggly record — stock prices, your heartbeat, a mountain skyline — can be rebuilt as a sum of waves. The rebuild always succeeds. So succeeding tells you nothing about what caused the wiggles. Writing a melody down in notes doesn’t mean a piano composed it. L&S11’s fit is bookkeeping presented as discovery.

Try it yourself · Exercise 1 Rebuild anything out of waves — a stock chart, a mountain skyline, twenty centuries of European population, or randomly generated data. The rebuild always succeeds.
Objection 2

Two and a half beats don’t make a rhythm

The whole record holds 160 years — fewer than three repeats of a 60‑year cycle, and the calibration window holds fewer than two. You cannot establish a rhythm from two beats. The tell: other periods fit the same data just as well — or better. Benestad et al. found that 65.75‑ and 21.5‑year waves fit HadCRUT3 better than 60 and 20. Strikingly, L&S11’s own paper concedes the point: they note that a free fit lands only “close to” 60, and the free‑fit target in their own supplement centers near 65 years — they imposed 60 anyway to match the astronomy. On NOAA’s data we find the best “long cycle” is ~87 years. When the discovered constant of nature changes every time you change the dataset — or disagrees with your own free fit — you haven’t discovered a constant of nature.

Try it yourself · Exercise 2 Turn the period dial — refit their model live on the real data and find the period that fits best. It is not 60.
Objection 3

The method finds cycles in pure randomness

This is the rebuttal’s centerpiece. Build a 10,000‑year artificial series out of hundreds of waves with random strengths and timings — structured noise with no special 60‑year cause. Slice it into 160‑year pieces, the length of the real record, and run L&S11’s fit on each slice. Every slice “finds” a 60‑year cycle. The apparent strength swings wildly from slice to slice, is inflated far above the series’ true 60‑year content (about four‑fold in Benestad et al.’s version; about an order of magnitude in ours — the exact factor depends on the noise recipe), and the strongest slices rival the real climate record. A smoke detector that goes off in every empty room tells you nothing when it goes off in yours.

Try it yourself · Exercise 3 Slice a random series — draw 160‑year slices of seeded noise, run their exact fit, and count how many times it reports no cycle. (It never does.)
Objection 4

Flexible models must face data they’ve never seen — this one didn’t

With enough adjustable parts, a model fits whatever it’s tuned to; the physicists’ joke, attributed to John von Neumann, is that with four parameters you can fit an elephant. Benestad et al.’s charge is that L&S11 never properly evaluated their model against independent data: the wiggle‑match through 2010 reuses the era that shaped the model’s human ramp, and the pre‑1950 “nature‑only” premise quietly assumes the very conclusion (a small early human effect) the paper set out to prove. The only clean test is data that arrived after publication. Hold that thought for Part Three.

Try it yourself · Exercise 4 Run the overfitting experiment — raise the wave count, fit the past as closely as you like, then reveal 2011–2025 and score the forecast.
Objection 5

The physics contradicts itself

To connect distant planets to Earth’s thermometers, the paper leans on resonance — the climate supposedly rings like a struck glass in sympathy with tiny astronomical nudges, while responding only sluggishly (a “slight negative feedback”) to the far larger energy push of added CO₂. But resonance is a property of the system, not of the forcing it prefers: a wine glass rings at its pitch for any sound at that pitch — the soprano, the cello, the crowd. Benestad et al. demonstrate with a simulated oscillator that a resonant system gets excited by noisy or trending pushes of any origin, greenhouse forcing included. A climate sensitive enough to amplify planetary whispers could not also be deaf to CO₂’s shout. And that exposes the deepest flaw — the false dichotomy: even if 20‑ and 60‑year wiggles are real (similar ocean variability appears in climate models too), wiggles ride on top of trends. Finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from CO₂. The paper never tested the greenhouse explanation it claimed to displace. One more structural weakness: the causal chain runs planets → solar activity → irradiance and cosmic‑ray/cloud effects → climate, and nearly every link is borrowed from contested sources — a solar‑irradiance history built on the solar‑cycle‑length idea, the disputed ACRIM satellite composite, and cosmic‑ray cloud seeding. The first and last of those are themselves examined and faulted elsewhere in the same replication study (the Friis‑Christensen & Lassen and Svensmark papers). The claim’s foundations cite each other more than they cite independent physics.

Try it yourself · Exercise 5 Push a resonant system — with a planetary metronome, with random noise, with a greenhouse ramp, or by hand. See whether it responds differently to any of them.
The verdict in the study’s taxonomy. Benestad et al. file L&S11 under logic type B — the machinery is applied, but the premises are wrong — with the shorthand label curve fitting: an over‑fit set of waves, no proper evaluation, implausible physics, and relevant literature ignored. Their analysis is reproducible via the open R package replicationDemos (function LoehleScafetta2011()).
PART THREE

The test nature ran anyway

Every forecast eventually meets the future. L&S11’s model says the 60‑year cycle has been in its downswing since ~2000, holding temperatures nearly flat — our faithful re‑run of their full model rises just 0.05 °C between 2000 and 2030 — on the way to less than 1 °C of warming for the whole century. Here is what the planet did instead.

This is the punchline the rebuttal predicted in advance: a curve fitted to wiggles has no reason to keep working once the wiggles stop cooperating. The 2000s “plateau” that made the model look prophetic turned out to be exactly the kind of temporary fluctuation the mainstream literature said it was. The budget arithmetic is brutal: the paper’s published ceiling for the entire century was about 0.6 °C of warming above 2000. Comparing decade averages to smooth out El Niño swings, the world warmed 0.47 °C between 1996–2005 and 2016–2025 — roughly three‑quarters of that hundred‑year allowance, spent in the first two decades, with individual recent years already past the ceiling.

SIDE BY SIDE

The two arguments at a glance

Loehle & Scafetta 2011Benestad et al. 2016
What are the wiggles? Real 60‑ & 20‑year cycles of astronomical origin. What wave‑fitting always produces; other periods fit as well or better, and random data yields the same “cycles.”
The human share? At most a ramp of ≈0.66 °C/century emerging in 1942 — about half the IPCC’s attribution, possibly less if urban‑heat contamination inflates it; sensitivity ~1–1.5 °C per doubling. An artifact of assuming pre‑1950 warming was natural and calling the leftover “human” — the premise smuggles in the conclusion.
The physics? Planets pace the Sun; a resonant climate follows (via irradiance and perhaps cosmic rays/clouds); CO₂ meets a slight negative feedback. The forecast? Warming masked by the cycle until ~2040; ≈0.6 °C above 2000 by 2100, vs. the ~3 °C they attribute to IPCC projections. Curve fits have no predictive warrant — and two decades of observations have already consumed ~¾ of that century budget.
What would count as proof? The model’s match to 1951–2010. Only genuine out‑of‑sample skill, tested against data the model never touched. It failed that test.
ANTICIPATING THE COMEBACKS

Three defenses, three answers

“But it predicted the plateau!”

The strongest defense is that the 1951–2010 wiggle‑match was a successful prediction — the paper itself claims the post‑2000 flattening as evidence in hand. Two answers. First, matching the phase of one or two wiggles is weak evidence: the random slices in Exhibit D produce phase‑coherent, convincing‑looking wiggles all the time, and a fit anchored on the 1940s bump and 1970s dip is halfway to “predicting” a 2000s crest by arithmetic alone. Second, the model’s decisive, no‑excuses prediction — the flat decades after 2010 — is the one that failed. When a model’s successes are the flexible parts and its failures are the rigid parts, that is the signature of curve fitting.

“But they validated the method in their supplement!”

They did anticipate the too‑short‑record objection. Their Supplemental Information 1 takes the fitted cycles‑plus‑line curve as a true signal, sprinkles white noise on top, refits on 1850–1950 a thousand times, and shows the period is recovered (64.1 ± 2.6 years against a target of 64.9). But notice what that tests: if the world really is two fixed cycles plus a line plus white noise, the fit can find them. It says nothing about the reverse risk — that the fit “finds” cycles when none exist. Real climate noise isn’t white; it’s structured, with slow wandering built in. Feed the same fit structured randomness containing no fixed cycle — Exhibit D — and it reports convincing 60‑year cycles anyway. A validation that assumes the conclusion cannot defend it. (Note, too, the quiet detail: their own validation target sits near 65 years, not 60.)

“But proxy records show 60‑year cycles for centuries!”

The paper’s supporting studies show quasi‑oscillations in the 50–80‑year range, in particular regions, waxing and waning in strength — which is exactly what slow, structured natural variability looks like. That is not a fixed global clock. The wider contrarian cycle literature, surveyed in Benestad et al.’s Table S2, reports “dominant” periods from 6 to 2,804 years — claims that cannot all be true and are rarely reconciled with one another. And the deepest answer doesn’t change: ocean oscillations of similar size appear in standard climate models as internal variability, no planets required — and even a genuine quasi‑cycle would ride on top of the greenhouse trend, subtracting nothing from it.

FOR CONVERSATIONS

Five lines that carry the argument

  • Any wiggly line can be rebuilt out of waves — the rebuild always works, so it proves nothing about causes.
  • 160 years contains two and a half “60‑year cycles.” You can’t establish a rhythm from two and a half beats.
  • Run their exact method on random noise and it finds 60‑year cycles there too — often just as strong.
  • A climate that resonates to planetary whispers can’t be deaf to CO₂’s shout — resonance doesn’t pick sides.
  • Their ceiling for the whole century was about 0.6 °C above 2000 — and the first two decades spent three‑quarters of it.
PLAIN WORDS

Glossary

Curve fitting / overfitting
Tuning a flexible formula until it hugs past data. The tighter the hug with the more knobs, the less it says about the future — it has memorized, not understood.
Sinusoid (wave, harmonic)
The smooth, endlessly repeating up‑and‑down curve of pure rhythm. Fourier’s theorem: stack enough of them and you can reproduce any finite record exactly.
Residual
What’s left of the data after subtracting a model. L&S11’s entire human‑contribution estimate is a straight line drawn through a residual.
Out‑of‑sample test
Checking a model against data it never saw during tuning. The only honest exam for a flexible model — and the one nature administered after 2011.
R² (“R‑squared”)
A 0‑to‑1 score for how much of the data’s variation a model soaks up. High R² on the training data is cheap; high R² on unseen data is the real currency.
Resonance
A system’s tendency to respond strongly to pushes near its natural frequency — like a playground swing. It responds to any push at that tempo, whoever is pushing.
False dichotomy
“It’s natural cycles, therefore it isn’t CO₂.” Wiggles and trends coexist; demonstrating one does not subtract the other.
Attribution
The science of assigning causes to observed climate change — properly done with physics and multiple independent lines of evidence, not by labeling leftovers.

Where “60 and 20 years” come from
The periods are not fitted — they are imported from planetary astronomy. Jupiter and Saturn realign about every 19.86 years (the great conjunction), and three such beats return the conjunction to nearly the same region of sky in ≈60 years. Scafetta (2010) argued that global temperature shares spectral peaks near those periods with the Sun's wobble around the solar system's centre of mass, and that ocean rhythms like the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation keep similar time. L&S11 adopt 60 and 20 on that authority; everything else in Eq. (1) — heights, timings, line — is least-squares bookkeeping. Whether the data independently prefer those periods is exactly what Objection 2 and Exercise 2 test; whether the proposed physical link could work is Objection 5.

Methods & honesty notes

This draft has been checked line‑by‑line against the original preprint (arXiv:1206.5845, now in the project files). Exhibits B, C and E re‑run the L&S11 procedure — their Eq. (1), periods fixed at 60 and 20 years, amplitudes and phases free, linear term, calibration 1850–1950 — on NOAA’s NOAAGlobalTemp annual anomalies, 1850–2025, relative to the 20th‑century average. Our ordinary‑least‑squares sine/cosine formulation is algebraically equivalent to their fixed‑period cosine fit. Dataset vintage explains the numerical differences: our 60‑yr amplitude 0.100 °C vs. their 0.121 ± 0.016; 20‑yr 0.043 vs. 0.041 ± 0.016; cycle peaks ≈1997/1998 vs. their 1998.6/1999.7; calibration R² 0.46 vs. 0.53; residual ramp 0.81 °C/century crossing ~1947 vs. their 0.66 ± 0.08 (fitted 1950–2010) crossing 1942. Exhibit D reproduces the supporting material’s synthetic experiment: a 10,000‑year sum of ~400 waves with random strengths and timings, scaled to the real record’s detrended variability, sliced into 62 pieces of 160 years; the inflation factor (ours ~14×, theirs ~4×) depends on the noise recipe — the instability is the finding, not the exact factor. This differs deliberately from the paper’s own Supplemental test, which adds white noise to an assumed‑true cycle and therefore only measures recovery‑given‑truth, not the false‑alarm rate. All figures were generated for this project; the script, template and data ship alongside this document so every number can be rebuilt — in the same spirit of open replication the rebuttal championed.
