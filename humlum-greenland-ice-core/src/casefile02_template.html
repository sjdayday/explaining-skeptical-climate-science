The 4,000-Year
Window

How a 2011 paper found three long natural cycles in the most recent slice of a 50,000-year ice-core record — and what the fitted curve did when it met the 46,000 years the paper set aside.

The claim, in brief

Greenland’s GISP2 ice core shows that the recent past is governed by long natural cycles — about 2,800, 1,190, and 560 years — plausibly of solar and lunar origin. Fitted to the last 4,000 years, these cycles reproduce the record well and permit “testable forecasts of future climate”: natural variation, not greenhouse gases, is the dominant driver, and natural cooling lies ahead. A companion paper by the same authors forecast Svalbard’s annual mean to fall by 3.5 ± 2 °C over solar cycle 24.

The rebuttal, in brief

The same public data file continues for another ~46,000 years — 92% of the record’s span — and the paper set that part aside. Extend the fitted curve into the discarded era and it fails at once: a fit with a 0.3 °C error inside its window misses by 2–4 °C outside it, within the same climate epoch. A sum of waves can always be made to fit a finite stretch of data; the only honest test is data the fit never touched, and this fit had 46,000 years of it waiting.

PART ONE

The original argument, at its strongest

In 2011, Ole Humlum, Jan-Erik Solheim and Kjell Stordahl — a Norwegian trio of a physical geographer, an astrophysicist, and a statistician — published “Identifying natural contributions to late Holocene climate change” in Global and Planetary Change. The paper analyzed one of the most celebrated datasets in paleoclimate: the GISP2 ice core from the summit of the Greenland ice sheet, whose oxygen isotopes yield a temperature reconstruction reaching back through the entire last ice age.

The intuition

If climate is shaped by slow natural rhythms — the Sun’s long activity cycles, tidal cycles of the Moon — then a record spanning millennia is where they should appear. Find the rhythms, and two things follow: the recent warming can be placed in its natural context, and the rhythms can be extended forward as a forecast. The authors examined the most recent 4,000 years of the GISP2 series and, using wavelet analysis, identified three dominant long cycles with periods near 2,804, 1,186, and 556 years. (They ran the same machinery on the much shorter Svalbard thermometer record, finding cycles of 68.4, 25.7, and 16.8 years there.) Their stated reason for the 4,000-year window, as quoted in the rebuttal:

“[They] chose to focus on the most recent 4000 years of the GISP2 series, as the main thrust of [their] investigation is on climatic variations in the recent past and their potential for forecasting the near future.” HSS11a, as quoted in the Benestad et al. supporting material, p. 5 (pronouns adapted)

What they concluded

The cycles, the authors argued, are natural — plausibly linked to solar activity and lunar tidal rhythms — and they dominate the record. On that basis the paper offered what it called “testable forecasts of future climate,” extending the fitted cycles forward and estimating, in the rebuttal’s quotation, “a realistic forecasting time range of about 10–25% of the total record length.” The implication: the coming decades belong to the cycles’ downswing — natural cooling or stasis, not accelerating greenhouse warming.

The same author cluster put a number on it. In the companion paper in this case’s sources/ folder — “Solar Activity and Svalbard Temperatures,” which builds directly on HSS11a’s Svalbard cycles — they modeled Arctic temperatures against the length of the solar cycle, reported that “60 per cent of the annual and winter temperature variations are explained by solar activity,” and concluded:

“We predict an annual mean temperature decrease for Svalbard of 3.5 ± 2°C from solar cycle 23 to solar cycle 24 (2009–20) and a decrease in the winter temperature of ≈6°C.” Solheim, Stordahl & Humlum, Adv. Meteorology (2011), abstract
Why it persuaded people. It has the ingredients that made Case File 01’s paper persuasive, on a grander canvas: real, respected data; simple visible rhythms; and the confidence of a falsifiable forecast. A 50,000-year ice core feels like the deep context that thermometer records lack — and a paper saying “we found the natural cycles in it” borrows that depth.

PART TWO

The rebuttal: the window and what it left out

Benestad and colleagues re-ran the analysis in their 2016 replication study of 38 contrarian papers, filing this one under selective use of data. Their objections build on each other, and the centerpiece needs no statistics at all.

Objection 1

Most of the data was set aside

The GISP2 temperature file is a single public download spanning nearly 50,000 years. The paper used the most recent 4,000 — 8% of the record’s span — and its authors knew the rest well: the rebuttal notes that Humlum had co-authored a popular technical article the same year presenting exactly the part of the record that the paper set aside. The stated reason (an interest in “the recent past”) explains the choice of emphasis; it does not explain why 46,000 years of the same variable from the same instrument were not used for the one thing discarded data is perfect for — testing the model.

Objection 2

Extended into the discarded data, the fit fails at once

This is the rebuttal’s centerpiece, and it is Case File 01’s lesson applied with a larger hammer: the honest test of a fitted curve is data it never touched. Here the test data did not have to be awaited — it was sitting in the same file. The rebuttal extended the fitted three-cycle model backward into the discarded era, in two variants: keeping the fitted trend, and — to give the model its best chance — zeroing the trend. Within the calibration window the fit misses by 0.3 °C on average. Across the rest of the Holocene — the same interglacial climate, no ice-age excuse available — it misses by 4.5 °C (trend kept) or 2.4 °C (trend zeroed), and its errors are worse than knowing nothing but the average would be. As the rebuttal put it: the authors “did produce a testable forecast of the past climate” — and the past falsified it.

Objection 3

A sum of waves always fits — the finding is the fitting

Case File 01 established this in detail: any finite record can be rebuilt from a sum of sinusoids, so a good in-window fit carries no information about causes. The rebuttal makes the textbook point directly — a Fourier representation is valid only within the fitted interval and is “mathematically bound to extend periodic regularity” beyond it, with no predictive warrant. It adds a technical caution specific to this paper’s method: wavelet results near a record’s end-points are sensitive to subjective boundary-condition choices, and the end-point here is precisely where the paper’s forecast lives. (For a hands-on demonstration of waves fitting anything, see Case File 01’s Exercise 1 — and its Objection 1, which this objection is the sequel to.)

Objection 4

The physics would need the Sun and Moon to begin working 4,000 years ago

Taking the model at face value creates a physical absurdity that the rebuttal states plainly: if the three cycles are driven by the Sun and the Moon, the drivers did not exist only in the last 4,000 years. The extension test shows the cycles absent from the earlier Holocene — so either the Sun and Moon “had no effect on the Greenland temperature in the ea[r]ly part of the Holocene, but suddenly started to play a role for the last 4000 years,” or the cycles are not what the paper says they are. There is no third option in the paper. And as in Case File 01, a genuine natural cycle would in any case ride on top of greenhouse warming rather than replace it — finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from a trend.

Objection 5

One borehole, ending in 1855

Two facts about the record itself bound what any analysis of it can say about modern, global climate. First, GISP2 is one location — the summit of the Greenland ice sheet — and central-Greenland temperature is not global temperature; regional records swing harder than the global mean. Second, the reconstruction’s ages count back from 1950, and its youngest sample is ≈95 years before that: the series ends around 1855. A paper using it to put “the recent past” in context is working from a record that stops before essentially all of the modern warming occurred. Neither fact is hidden — both are in the data file’s own header — but neither survives into the claim’s framing.

The verdict in the study’s taxonomy. Benestad et al. class the paper between logic types B (wrong premises) and D (incomplete premises), under the heading selective use of data: the discarded 46,000 years were never used to evaluate the model, the fit is a Fourier exercise with no predictive warrant, and the proposed physics is inconsistent with the record it was fitted to. Their analysis is reproducible via the open R package replicationDemos (function Humlum.et.al.2011()), and this case file’s figures re-run it on the same NOAA data file.
PART THREE

What happened after publication

The GISP2 paper’s forecast content was directional — natural cycles implying cooling or stasis ahead. But the companion Svalbard paper made the cluster’s prediction concrete and dated: an annual-mean drop of 3.5 ± 2 °C at Svalbard from solar cycle 23 to solar cycle 24 (given as 2009–20), and winters colder by about 6 °C. Solar cycle 24 has now come and gone. Svalbard did not cool by three and a half degrees; the archipelago instead recorded some of the fastest warming measured anywhere on Earth over precisely that period, with record-warm years in the middle of the forecast window. A quantitative verification exhibit from the Svalbard Airport station series is planned for the next draft (see NOTES.md); the qualitative verdict is not in doubt.

The deeper aftermath is the one the record itself supplies. The claim’s framing — put recent climate in the context of natural cycles — used a record that ends around 1855. The century and a half of instrumental data since, the part where the anthropogenic signal lives, is exactly the part the ice core cannot see. And the part of the ice core the paper could see but set aside is exactly the part that falsifies its model. Between the two exclusions, the conclusion was never at risk from any data the paper examined.

SIDE BY SIDE

The two arguments at a glance

QuestionHumlum, Solheim & Stordahl 2011Benestad et al. 2016
What was analyzed? The most recent 4,000 years of the GISP2 record, by wavelet analysis; three cycles (≈2,804, 1,186, 556 yr) identified as dominant. The same file continues ~46,000 years further back; 92% of the record’s span was set aside without being used to test the model.
What does the fit show? The three cycles plus a trend reproduce the window well and support “testable forecasts of future climate.” In-window fit R² 0.85 (RMSE 0.3 °C) collapses to RMSE 2.4–4.5 °C in the discarded Holocene — worse than using the average. A Fourier sum always fits its own window.
The physics? The cycles are plausibly solar and lunar in origin. Then they should exist before 4,000 years ago — and the discarded data shows they do not. The proposed drivers cannot have switched on recently.
The forecast? Natural cooling ahead; companion paper: Svalbard −3.5 ± 2 °C over solar cycle 24. Svalbard instead warmed rapidly through cycle 24 — among the fastest warming measured anywhere.
The record’s reach? Used to put recent climate change in natural context. One central-Greenland site, youngest sample ≈1855 CE: the record stops before essentially all modern warming.
IN BRIEF

The argument in five sentences

  • The paper fitted three long cycles to the most recent 4,000 years of a ~50,000-year data file — and set the other 92% aside.
  • Discarded data is a ready-made test: extend the fit into it, and the error grows roughly ten-fold within the same climate epoch.
  • A sum of waves can always be made to fit a finite stretch of data; only data the fit never touched can validate it (Case File 01’s lesson, repeated).
  • If the cycles were really solar and lunar, they could not exist only in the last 4,000 years — yet outside the window, they don’t.
  • The record itself ends around 1855 at one spot in Greenland — before essentially all modern warming — and the same team’s dated forecast (Svalbard −3.5 °C over solar cycle 24) was falsified by rapid warming instead.

Methods & reproducibility

All figures use the GISP2 temperature reconstruction (Alley 2000; data citation Alley 2004, NOAA NCEI Paleoclimatology), downloaded 2026-08-09 from the same file cited by the rebuttal’s supporting material (footnote 2): 1,631 samples, 0.095–49.98 kyr before 1950. The fitted model is a constant + linear trend + three sinusoids with periods fixed at 2,804/1,186/556 years (amplitudes and phases by least squares on ages ≤ 4 kyr BP), replicating the supporting material’s Humlum.et.al.2011() demonstration; HSS11a’s own analysis was wavelet-based, and our coefficients are not presented as theirs. In-window: R² 0.852, RMSE 0.30 °C. Extension over 4–11.7 kyr BP: RMSE 4.45 °C (trend kept), 2.39 °C (trend zeroed). Every number regenerates from src/build.py (deterministic; no random numbers in this case).
