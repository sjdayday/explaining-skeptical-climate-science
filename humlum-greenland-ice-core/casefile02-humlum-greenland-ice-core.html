The 4,000-Year
Window

How a 2011 paper found three long natural cycles in the most recent slice of a 50,000-year ice-core record — and what the fitted curve did when it met the 46,000 years the paper set aside.

The claim, in brief

Greenland’s GISP2 ice core shows that the recent past is governed by long natural cycles — 2,804, 1,186, and 556 years — plausibly of solar and lunar origin. Fitted to the last 4,000 years, three cycles reproduce every major warm and cold period (r² = 0.63) and permit “testable forecast of future climate”: the warming since the Little Ice Age “to a high degree may be the result of natural climatic variations.” For Svalbard, the same machinery forecast temperatures declining until about 2015–2017; a companion paper put it at −3.5 ± 2 °C over solar cycle 24.

The rebuttal, in brief

The same public data file continues for another ~46,000 years — 92% of the record’s span — and the paper set that part aside. Extend the fitted curve into the discarded era and it fails at once: a fit with a 0.3 °C error inside its window misses by 2–4 °C outside it, within the same climate epoch. A sum of waves can always be made to fit a finite stretch of data; the only honest test is data the fit never touched, and this fit had 46,000 years of it waiting.

PART ONE

The original argument, at its strongest

In 2011, Ole Humlum, Jan-Erik Solheim and Kjell Stordahl — a Norwegian trio of a physical geographer, an astrophysicist, and a statistician — published “Identifying natural contributions to late Holocene climate change” in Global and Planetary Change. The paper analyzed one of the most celebrated datasets in paleoclimate: the GISP2 ice core from the summit of the Greenland ice sheet, whose oxygen isotopes yield a temperature reconstruction reaching back through the entire last ice age.

The intuition

If climate is shaped by slow natural rhythms — the Sun’s long activity cycles, tidal cycles of the Moon — then a record spanning millennia is where they should appear. Find the rhythms, and two things follow: the recent warming can be placed in its natural context, and the rhythms can be extended forward as a forecast. The paper presents itself as “an empirical bottom-up approach to climate modelling,” in deliberate contrast to physics-based climate models: instead of solving the equations of the atmosphere, decompose real temperature records into their periodic components — Fourier analysis for the spectrum, wavelet analysis (the Morlet transform, AutoSignal v1.7 software) to see how each rhythm’s strength varies over time — then keep the few dominant, persistent cycles as a model, fitted by nonlinear optimization.

What they did

They applied the machinery to two Arctic records. On the Svalbard thermometer series (1912–2010, the High Arctic’s longest), the spectrum is dominated by periods of 68.4, 25.7 and 16.8 years, and a three-cycle model hindcasts the record with r² = 0.47 — against 0.14 for a straight line. Their strongest card is a real out-of-sample test: truncate the series at 1990, refit, and the model (periods 71.7, 24.9, 15.3 yr) tracks the withheld 1991–2010 years — including the warm 2005–2007 — about as well as it tracked the training years. From tests like this they estimate “a realistic forecasting time range of about 10–25% of the total record length.”

For deep context they turned to the GISP2 ice core, taking the most recent 4,000 years of the temperature reconstruction. Their stated reason, in full:

“Here we chose to focus on the most recent 4000 years of the GISP2 series, as the main thrust of our investigation is on climatic variations in the recent past and their potential for forecasting the near future. In addition, this part of the GISP2 series shows an overall linear temperature trend, which simplifies the following analysis.” HSS11a, Global and Planetary Change 79, pp. 145–146

On that window, after removing a small linear trend, their Fourier spectrum shows five periods — 3,598, 1,139, 788, 584 and 366 years — all exceeding the 99.9% significance level against white noise. Nonlinear optimization then settled on a three-period model of 2,804, 1,186 and 556 years, which hindcasts the window with r² = 0.63 and reproduces, in their words, the timing of the Medieval, Roman and Minoan warm periods and the intervening Dark Age and Little Ice Age cold spells. The paper’s candidate mechanisms are lunar tidal cycles (the 18.6-year nodal cycle and its harmonics, following Yndestad) and known solar cycles (the ~205-year De Vries and ~2,300-year Hallstatt cycles), while noting that “the attribution of physical explanations for the observed cyclic climate change is not our main concern.”

What they concluded

Because the record “ends 1855 AD, before onset of the 20th century warming,” the authors read the forward extension of their cycles as a further test — and a success: “the natural cycle approach based on wavelet analysis is able to forecast the observed 1856–2010 warming (Fig. 8) as an out-of-sample forecasting test” (155 years, they note, being about 4% of the record’s length). From this they concluded that “the present period of warming since the LIA to a high degree may be the result of natural climatic variations, known to characterise at least the previous 4000 years,” and — extending forward — that “the present post LIA warm period is likely to continue for most of the 21st century,” after which the late-Holocene cooling trend may resume, “dependant on the magnitude of the anthropogenic greenhouse enhancement.” For Svalbard, their model forecast temperatures declining to about 2015–2017, then a minor warm peak around 2026: “the observed late 20th century warming in Svalbard is not going to continue for the next 20–25 years.”

The same author cluster put a sharper number on the Svalbard forecast. In the companion paper in this case’s sources/ folder — “Solar Activity and Svalbard Temperatures,” which builds directly on HSS11a’s Svalbard analysis — they modeled Arctic temperatures against the length of the solar cycle, reported that “60 per cent of the annual and winter temperature variations are explained by solar activity,” and concluded:

“We predict an annual mean temperature decrease for Svalbard of 3.5 ± 2°C from solar cycle 23 to solar cycle 24 (2009–20) and a decrease in the winter temperature of ≈6°C.” Solheim, Stordahl & Humlum, Adv. Meteorology (2011), abstract
Why it persuaded people. It has the ingredients that made Case File 01’s paper persuasive, on a grander canvas: real, respected data; simple visible rhythms; a genuine out-of-sample test on the Svalbard record; and the confidence of a falsifiable forecast. A 50,000-year ice core feels like the deep context that thermometer records lack — and a paper saying “we found the natural cycles in it” borrows that depth. The paper is also candid in places its critics are not obliged to be: it flags the record’s 1855 endpoint, notes that central-Greenland temperature “is not identical to global temperature,” and concedes its Svalbard trend may be “unrealistic high.” The question Part Two asks is whether the candour reaches the one comparison that matters.
PART TWO

The rebuttal: the window and what it left out

Benestad and colleagues re-ran the analysis in their 2016 replication study of 38 contrarian papers, filing this one under selective use of data. Their objections build on each other, and the centerpiece needs no statistics at all.

Objection 1

Most of the data was set aside

The GISP2 temperature file is a single public download spanning nearly 50,000 years. The paper used the most recent 4,000 — 8% of the record’s span — and its authors knew the rest well: the rebuttal notes that Humlum had co-authored a popular technical article the same year presenting exactly the part of the record that the paper set aside. The paper’s two stated reasons deserve separate weighing. An interest in “the recent past” explains the choice of emphasis; it does not explain why 46,000 years of the same variable from the same instrument were never used for the one thing discarded data is perfect for — testing the model. The second reason is more revealing: the window was chosen partly because it “shows an overall linear temperature trend, which simplifies the following analysis” — that is, the data was selected for being the kind of data the model handles well.

Objection 2

Extended into the discarded data, the fit fails at once

This is the rebuttal’s centerpiece, and it is Case File 01’s lesson applied with a larger hammer: the honest test of a fitted curve is data it never touched. The paper itself claims such a test — it reads the match between its extended cycles and the warming after the record’s 1855 endpoint as “an out-of-sample forecasting test” passed. But that comparison spans 155 years against a model whose shortest cycle is 556 years: at that resolution, “forecasting the 1856–2010 warming” means only that the fitted long cycles were rising at the window’s edge. The test with real teeth was sitting in the same file, and did not have to be awaited: 7,700 more years of the same interglacial climate. The rebuttal extended the model backward, in two variants — keeping the fitted trend, and, to give the model its best chance, zeroing it. Within the window the fit misses by 0.30 °C on average. Within the first 1,000 years outside it — inside the paper’s own claimed forecasting range of “10–25% of the total record length” — the miss is already 1.0–1.7 °C, three to five times worse. Across the rest of the Holocene it reaches 2.4–4.5 °C, worse than knowing nothing but the average. As the rebuttal put it: the authors “did produce a testable forecast of the past climate” — and the past falsified it.

Objection 3

A sum of waves always fits — the finding is the fitting

Case File 01 established this in detail: any finite record can be rebuilt from a sum of sinusoids, so a good in-window fit carries no information about causes. The rebuttal makes the textbook point directly — a Fourier representation is valid only within the fitted interval and is “mathematically bound to extend periodic regularity” beyond it, with no predictive warrant. Two details of the paper sharpen the point here. Its longest “cycle,” 2,804 years, is 70% of the window’s length — the window contains 1.4 repeats of it, and a rhythm cannot be established from a beat and a half (Case File 01’s Objection 2, at larger scale). And 2,804 appears nowhere in the paper’s own Fourier spectrum, whose long peak sits at 3,598 years; the optimizer moved it. The rebuttal adds a technical caution specific to the method: wavelet results near a record’s end-points are sensitive to subjective boundary-condition choices, and the end-point is precisely where the forecast lives. (For a hands-on demonstration of waves fitting anything, see Case File 01’s Exercise 1 — and its Objection 1, which this objection is the sequel to.)

Objection 4

The physics would need the Sun and Moon to begin working 4,000 years ago

Taking the model at face value creates a physical absurdity that the rebuttal states plainly: if the three cycles are driven by the Sun and the Moon, the drivers did not exist only in the last 4,000 years. The extension test shows the cycles absent from the earlier Holocene — so either the Sun and Moon “had no effect on the Greenland temperature in the ea[r]ly part of the Holocene, but suddenly started to play a role for the last 4000 years,” or the cycles are not what the paper says they are. There is no third option in the paper. And as in Case File 01, a genuine natural cycle would in any case ride on top of greenhouse warming rather than replace it — finding a wiggle subtracts nothing from a trend.

Objection 5

One borehole, ending in 1855

Two facts about the record itself bound what any analysis of it can say about modern, global climate. First, GISP2 is one location — the summit of the Greenland ice sheet — and central-Greenland temperature is not global temperature; regional records swing harder than the global mean. Second, the reconstruction’s ages count back from 1950, and its youngest sample is ≈95 years before that: the series ends around 1855. To the paper’s credit, it states both facts — the 1855 endpoint explicitly, and the caveat that central-Greenland changes “are not identical to global temperature changes,” though it adds that they “tend to reflect planetary temperature changes with a decadal-scale delay.” The objection is that the caveats do not survive into the conclusions: a record that stops before essentially all of the modern warming is used to conclude that the warming “to a high degree may be the result of natural climatic variations,” and a model whose shortest cycle is 556 years — coarser than the entire instrumental era — is read as having forecast that era’s behaviour.

The verdict in the study’s taxonomy. Benestad et al. class the paper between logic types B (wrong premises) and D (incomplete premises), under the heading selective use of data: the discarded 46,000 years were never used to evaluate the model, the fit is a Fourier exercise with no predictive warrant, and the proposed physics is inconsistent with the record it was fitted to. Their analysis is reproducible via the open R package replicationDemos (function Humlum.et.al.2011()), and this case file’s figures re-run it on the same NOAA data file.
PART THREE

What happened after publication

The paper made two dated predictions of its own. For Svalbard: temperatures declining until about 2015–2017, a minor peak around 2026, and — in its words — “the observed late 20th century warming in Svalbard is not going to continue for the next 20–25 years.” For central Greenland: the post-Little-Ice-Age warm period continuing for most of the 21st century, at cycle-scale resolution. The companion Svalbard paper made the cluster’s prediction concrete and falsifiable on a decade: an annual-mean drop of 3.5 ± 2 °C at Svalbard from solar cycle 23 to solar cycle 24 (given as 2009–20), and winters colder by about 6 °C. Solar cycle 24 has now come and gone. Svalbard did not cool by three and a half degrees, and the late-20th-century warming did continue: the archipelago instead recorded some of the fastest warming measured anywhere on Earth over precisely that period, with record-warm years in the middle of the forecast window. A quantitative verification exhibit from the Svalbard Airport station series is planned for the next draft (see NOTES.md); the qualitative verdict is not in doubt.

The deeper aftermath is the one the record itself supplies. The claim’s framing — put recent climate in the context of natural cycles — used a record that ends around 1855. The century and a half of instrumental data since, the part where the anthropogenic signal lives, is exactly the part the ice core cannot see. And the part of the ice core the paper could see but set aside is exactly the part that falsifies its model. Between the two exclusions, the conclusion was never at risk from any data the paper examined.

IN BRIEF

The argument in five sentences

  • The paper fitted three long cycles to the most recent 4,000 years of a ~50,000-year data file — and set the other 92% aside.
  • Discarded data is a ready-made test: extend the fit into it, and the error triples within the paper’s own claimed forecasting range, growing roughly ten-fold across the rest of the same climate epoch.
  • A sum of waves can always be made to fit a finite stretch of data; only data the fit never touched can validate it (Case File 01’s lesson, repeated).
  • If the cycles were really solar and lunar, they could not exist only in the last 4,000 years — yet outside the window, they don’t.
  • The record itself ends around 1855 at one spot in Greenland — before essentially all modern warming — and the same team’s dated forecast (Svalbard −3.5 °C over solar cycle 24) was falsified by rapid warming instead.

Methods & reproducibility

All figures use the GISP2 temperature reconstruction (Alley 2000; data citation Alley 2004, NOAA NCEI Paleoclimatology), downloaded 2026-08-09 from the same file cited by the rebuttal’s supporting material (footnote 2): 1,631 samples, 0.095–49.98 kyr before 1950. The fitted model is a constant + linear trend + three sinusoids with periods fixed at 2,804/1,186/556 years (amplitudes and phases by least squares on ages ≤ 4 kyr BP), replicating the supporting material’s Humlum.et.al.2011() demonstration; HSS11a’s own model was built by wavelet-guided nonlinear optimization (reported r² 0.63) and our coefficients are not presented as theirs. In-window: R² 0.852, RMSE 0.30 °C. Extension over 4–5 kyr BP (the first 1,000 years, within the paper’s stated 10–25%-of-record forecasting range): RMSE 1.66 °C (trend kept), 1.02 °C (trend zeroed). Extension over the full 4–11.7 kyr BP: RMSE 4.45 °C and 2.39 °C respectively. Quotations were verified against the primary PDF in sources/ (page numbers are the journal’s, 145–156). Every number regenerates from src/build.py (deterministic; no random numbers in this case).
